What Cupping Therapy Actually Does

Most hands-on therapy works by pushing into tissue. Cupping does the opposite: a cup creates negative pressure that lifts the skin, fascia and the superficial muscle layer away from what's underneath. That's why it's often described as myofascial decompression — it decompresses tissue that has become stuck down, rather than compressing it further.

Lifting that layer does two things at once. It separates fascial planes that have started to adhere and stopped gliding over each other, and it pulls fresh blood into an area where local circulation has gone sluggish. That combination is why cupping is so often used for the heavy, dull, "stuck" quality of pain that doesn't fully respond to massage or stretching.

At GreenLeaf, cupping is rarely a standalone gimmick. It's the tool we reach for when an area is tight across a broad region, when circulation in one spot is clearly restricted, or when needling has released the muscle but the surrounding fascia is still holding on.

The Three Kinds of Cupping We Use

"Cupping" covers several quite different techniques. Which one we use depends on the tissue involved and what your symptoms are doing — not on preference.

Type 01 Fire Cupping (Dry Cupping)

The classic technique. A brief flame heats the air inside a glass cup, and as that air cools the cup is placed on the skin and creates suction — no flame ever touches you. Cups are left in place for several minutes over tight muscle groups. This is the version most people picture when they search for cupping, and it's the one we start with for broad, general tightness.

Broad tightness across the back or shoulders
A heavy, fatigued feeling in the muscle
Stiffness that returns a day or two after massage
First-time cupping patients
Treatment focus

Decompress a wide band of tight muscle and draw circulation into it, usually after or alongside acupuncture in the same visit.

Type 02 Sliding (Moving) Cupping

Oil is applied to the skin and the cup is glided along the muscle while suction is maintained, instead of being parked in one spot. It covers far more surface area than stationary cups and is better suited to long muscle groups — the erector spinae along the spine, the upper trapezius, the IT band. Patients usually describe it as a deep, dragging pull rather than pressure.

Tightness running the length of the back
Restriction across a broad fascial sheet
Post-workout or post-shift muscle fatigue
Tension without one clearly painful point
Treatment focus

Free up fascia across a whole region so the muscles underneath can lengthen and glide normally again.

Type 03 Wet Cupping

Used when one specific area feels heavy and local circulation is clearly stagnant. A very small skin-level stimulation is made first, then the cup is applied to draw out a small amount of congested blood from that spot. Internationally this technique is also known as hijama. It's the most targeted of the three and, in pain cases, often pairs with acupotomy — a fine bladed needle used to release fascial adhesion — where scarring or adhesion around a ligament or tendon won't resolve with needling alone.

One area that feels heavy, dull and stuck
Pain that keeps returning to the same fixed spot
Long-standing ligament or tendon injury
Nerve-compression pain at the base of the skull
Treatment focus

Clear stagnant local circulation at a specific site, and restore blood flow to tissue that has been restricted long enough to stop healing on its own.

What a Cupping Session Looks Like Here

A cupping visit at our Granville Street clinic follows the same sequence every time, so you know what's coming.

Step 01 Assessment

We talk through where the pain is, what makes it worse, and how long it has been there — then palpate the area to find out whether we're dealing with muscle, fascia or circulation. That's what decides whether you get fire, sliding or wet cupping, and whether cupping is the right tool at all.

Step 02 Warming the tissue

A TDP heat lamp, hot-stone work or acupressure is often used first to relax the surface muscle, which makes the deeper layer easier to reach and the cupping itself more comfortable.

Step 03 Cupping

Cups are placed — or glided — over the target area. You'll feel a firm pulling or tight sensation, not a sharp one. Stationary cups usually stay on for a few minutes, and your practitioner checks in on the intensity throughout.

Step 04 Aftercare

We go over what the marks mean, what to avoid for the next day, and the stretching or movement work that keeps the area from tightening back up before your next visit.

Cupping Marks Aren't Bruises

The circular marks left after cupping are the part patients ask about most. They aren't impact bruises. Suction draws blood toward the surface in an area where circulation was already restricted, so the colour reflects the state of the tissue underneath — a spot with poor local circulation tends to mark more darkly than a healthy one, and the same person will mark less over a course of treatment as circulation improves.

Dry and sliding cupping marks typically fade within a few days to about a week. Marking after wet cupping generally fades within 3 to 10 days, though it varies by person and can take up to two weeks. They aren't painful, and wet cupping does not leave a scar.

For about 24 hours afterward, it's best to skip heavy alcohol, saunas and hot baths, keep the area covered and warm, and drink plenty of water. Light movement is fine; a hard workout the same day isn't ideal.

When Cupping Isn't the Right Choice

Tell us before treatment if any of these apply. Cupping is a low-risk treatment, but it isn't for every person or every area.

Wet cupping and stronger acupotomy techniques are generally avoided during pregnancy, and treatment area and intensity are adjusted based on your stage of pregnancy — so please let us know as soon as you know. We also need to know about blood-clotting disorders and blood-thinning medication, since these change how tissue responds to suction.

Cups are not placed over broken or inflamed skin, active rashes, recent surgical sites, varicose veins, or areas of unexplained swelling. If you're being treated for a bleeding disorder or severe anemia, we'll use other methods instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does cupping therapy cost in Vancouver?

At GreenLeaf, cupping therapy is $115 for an initial visit and $95 for follow-ups. If cupping is delivered as part of a Pain Management Complex Acupuncture treatment, that visit is $155 initial and $135 follow-up, and can also include electro-acupuncture, trigger-point work, hot-stone massage or tui-na.

Does cupping hurt?

It shouldn’t. Most people describe a firm pulling or tight sensation while the cups are on, and sliding cupping feels like a deep drag along the muscle — neither is sharp pain. Suction strength is adjustable, so tell your practitioner at any point if it feels like too much and it will be reduced.

How long do cupping marks last?

Marks from dry or sliding cupping usually fade within a few days to about a week. Marking after wet cupping generally fades within 3 to 10 days, and occasionally up to two weeks. Darker marking in one spot usually means circulation in that area was more restricted to begin with, and the same area typically marks less as treatment progresses.

What’s the difference between dry cupping and wet cupping?

Dry cupping (including fire cupping) uses suction alone to lift and decompress tissue. Wet cupping adds a very small skin-level stimulation before the cup is applied, so a small amount of congested blood is drawn out of that specific spot. Dry cupping suits broad muscle tightness; wet cupping is used when one area feels heavy and local circulation is clearly stagnant.

Is cupping covered by MSP, ICBC, or extended health insurance in BC?

MSP does not cover cupping. MSP supplementary benefits apply to acupuncture only ($23 per visit, no doctor’s referral needed, up to a combined annual limit of 10 visits across several therapies), so a cupping treatment isn’t billable to MSP. ICBC and extended health are different: after a motor vehicle accident ICBC covers treatment regardless of fault and we bill ICBC directly, and most extended health plans cover cupping as part of your treatment — we direct-bill more than 35 insurers. Plan rules vary, so it’s worth confirming the specifics with your provider.

How many cupping sessions will I need?

It depends on how long the problem has been there. Recent muscle tightness often eases within one to three sessions. Long-standing restriction, tendon or ligament adhesion, and post-accident recovery usually need steadier care — roughly one to two sessions a week over several weeks — and we reassess the plan as the tissue changes.

Can I get cupping while pregnant?

Let us know as soon as you know you’re pregnant. Wet cupping and stronger acupotomy techniques are generally avoided during pregnancy, and the treatment area and intensity are adjusted based on your stage of pregnancy and overall condition.

What should I avoid after a cupping session?

For about 24 hours, skip heavy alcohol, saunas and hot baths, keep the treated area covered and warm, and drink plenty of water. Light movement and normal daily activity are fine; a hard workout the same day is better postponed.

Do you do cupping and acupuncture in the same visit?

Often, yes. Cupping is usually combined with acupuncture, electroacupuncture or acupotomy rather than used on its own, because each addresses a different tissue problem — muscle, fascia, adhesion or circulation. Which combination you get is decided after the assessment at the start of your visit.
