Headache Treatment, Matched to What's Actually Causing It

A headache is rarely just one thing. Muscle tension, an overexcited nervous system, hormonal shifts, and pinched nerves can all be involved — often at once. Traditional Eastern Medicine care starts by figuring out which of these is driving your pattern, then treats accordingly.

Why Headaches Rarely Have One Simple Cause

A headache is rarely just "my head hurts." Most of the time it's what you feel when several things are happening together — tight muscles, an overexcited nervous system, hormonal shifts, and nerve compression are the most common contributors, and they frequently overlap. Tension headaches and migraines showing up in the same person, even the same week, is especially common.

Because acupuncture calms an overactive nervous system and supports hormonal balance, in addition to releasing tight muscles, it can address more than one of these contributors at the same time.

Muscle Tension

Ongoing tension in the jaw, neck, shoulder, and scalp muscles creates a band-like pressure around the head. It tends to build through the day and peak with stress or fatigue.

Nervous System Overactivity

A nervous system that's become overly sensitive produces the throbbing, one-sided pain typical of migraine, often with nausea or sensitivity to light and sound.

Hormonal Changes

Headaches frequently intensify around your period, after childbirth, or during menopause — times when hormone levels shift significantly.

Nerve Compression

When a sensory nerve at the base of the skull gets compressed by surrounding muscle, it produces sharp, stabbing, or electric pain along the nerve's path.

How Acupuncture Addresses Headaches

Acupuncture doesn't just target the sore spot — it works on the muscular, nervous, and hormonal systems at the same time.

01

Releasing Muscle Tension

Most headache patients carry trigger points — tight, tender knots — in the muscles around the head, jaw, neck, and shoulders. Needling these points relaxes the muscle spasm and improves local blood flow. The muscles at the base of the skull, upper trapezius, temples, and jaw matter most.

Calming an Overactive Nervous System

Migraine involves both overexcitability in certain brain regions and an oversensitive nerve-vascular response around the face and head. Acupuncture influences the release of serotonin, endorphins, and GABA in the central nervous system — calming that overactivity and raising your threshold for pain, rather than only relaxing muscle.

The Six Headache Patterns We See in Clinic

The same word — "headache" — can describe very different underlying patterns. Recognizing which one (or which mix) applies to you shapes how we treat it.

Tension Headache

Muscle

A band-like pressure around the whole head, often worse in the afternoon or after stress. Chronic tightness in the jaw, neck, and shoulder muscles is the main driver — not usually severe, but persistent and easily becomes chronic.

Approach: acupuncture directly releasing the tight muscles, paired with electroacupuncture.

Migraine

Nerve & Vascular

Throbbing, one-sided pain, often with nausea, vomiting, or sensitivity to light and sound. Some people notice flashing lights beforehand. Closely tied to overexcitability in the nerves and blood vessels of the head and face, and to hormonal shifts.

Approach: acupuncture and electroacupuncture to calm the nervous system, with needling around the scalp, neck, and ears.

Mixed Tension-Migraine

Muscle & Nerve

Everyday neck and shoulder tightness that shifts into throbbing, one-sided pain when fatigue builds or around your period. In practice, this combination is very common — muscle tension and nerve overactivity are both active at once.

Approach: muscle relaxation and autonomic nervous system regulation, addressed together in the same course of care.

Nerve Compression Headache (Occipital Neuralgia)

Nerve Compression

A sharp, burning, or electric pain on one side of the back of the head, caused by a sensory nerve getting compressed as it passes through the muscles at the base of the skull. Touching your hair or the back of your head can trigger it, and pressing the area often reproduces the pain. Usually comes with chronic neck and shoulder tension.

Approach: Acupotomy to release adhesions around the compressed nerve, and Wet Cupping to clear stagnant blood and inflammation and decompress the area — muscle release alone is often not enough here.

TMJ-Related Headache

Jaw

Headaches driven by clenching, jaw-resting habits, or teeth grinding, which tighten the chewing and temple muscles. Clicking sounds or a jaw that doesn't open fully can come along with it.

Approach: needling around the jaw joint, sometimes including intraoral points, together with habit-correction guidance.

Hormonal / Menstrual Headache

Hormonal

Headaches that appear or worsen around your period, after childbirth, or during menopause — periods when hormone levels shift sharply and, in turn, affect the autonomic nervous system.

Approach: acupuncture to stabilize the hormone-regulating axis and autonomic nervous system, with treatment timing adjusted to your cycle.

Tracing the Muscles Behind Your Headache

Headaches usually come from the muscles around the jaw, neck, shoulders, and scalp rather than the head itself. That's why every visit includes checking these muscles one by one for tenderness, tightness, and adhesion — in the same order, every time.

Step 1 Jaw Joint
Masseter Temporalis Medial & Lateral Pterygoids

Tightened by clenching or resting the jaw — refers pain to the temple, cheek, and jaw, and deeper involvement can bring jaw clicking or locking.

Step 2 Front & Side of Neck
Sternocleidomastoid Scalenes Longus Colli

Tightness here can refer pain to the temple, around the eye, and the forehead, and can overlap with arm tingling. Easily overworked by a forward-head posture.

Step 3 Back of Neck & Shoulder
Suboccipitals Splenius Capitis / Cervicis Upper Trapezius Levator Scapulae

The key area for nerve-compression headaches — the suboccipital muscles refer pain from the base of the skull up to the crown. Upper trapezius is the classic tension-headache muscle.

Step 4 Scalp
Frontalis Occipitalis Temporal Fan Muscles

Tension here creates a heavy, pulling feeling across the forehead and scalp — often the last layer to check once deeper tension has been addressed.

Simple tight spots respond to acupuncture; chronic or highly reactive areas get electroacupuncture; adhesions or nerve compression are addressed with Acupotomy; areas of stagnant local blood flow get Wet Cupping; and jaw imbalance is treated with acupuncture around the joint plus habit-correction guidance.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many sessions does headache treatment usually take?

Acute headaches often improve within 1-3 sessions. Chronic tension headaches, migraines, and nerve-compression headaches usually need steadier care — around 1-2 sessions a week for 4-8 weeks or more. For frequent migraine attacks, preventive treatment matters most, and we adjust the frequency and focus of care based on your headache diary.

Can acupuncture help prevent migraines, not just relieve an attack?

Yes. Beyond easing an active attack, acupuncture can help reduce how often and how intensely migraines occur. It works by calming an overactive nervous system and stabilizing hormonal and autonomic function, which raises the threshold before a migraine starts. Preventive benefit builds with consistent treatment over time.

What is Acupotomy and how is it used for headaches?

Acupotomy uses a fine needle with a small blade tip to release adhesions where a nerve is compressed, or to separate fascia that has stuck together and stopped gliding properly. For headaches, it's most often used at the base of the skull and where the neck and upper trapezius muscles attach, to relieve nerve compression and restore blood flow.

Does the bruising from Wet Cupping last a long time?

Bruising from Wet Cupping usually fades within 3-10 days, though this varies by person — darker bruising often reflects an area with more restricted blood flow to begin with. For headaches, it's typically applied to the back of the head, neck, and shoulders. We recommend avoiding heavy alcohol, saunas, and hot baths for about 24 hours afterward.

Is it safe to get headache treatment during pregnancy?

Headaches are common in pregnancy due to hormonal and circulatory changes, and acupuncture can generally be provided safely. Please let us know as soon as you know you're pregnant, since we adjust the treatment area and intensity based on your stage of pregnancy — Wet Cupping and stronger Acupotomy work are typically avoided during pregnancy.

Can I keep taking painkillers while getting acupuncture treatment?

In most cases the two can be combined, and many patients find they need less medication, less often, as treatment progresses. Please tell us about any medications you're taking, especially migraine-preventive drugs, blood pressure medication, or antidepressants, so we can factor them into your treatment plan.

Would keeping a headache diary actually help?

Yes — tracking the date, time, intensity, and duration of each headache, along with your cycle, sleep, stress, food, weather, and any medication taken, makes it much easier to identify your specific triggers and patterns. We use that information to fine-tune the timing and focus of your treatment, and to give you more specific lifestyle guidance.
