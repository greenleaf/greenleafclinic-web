Elbow Pain

"Tennis elbow" and "golf elbow" describe where the pain shows up, not what's actually wrong. The same ache can come from a tight muscle, a pinched nerve, a stiff joint, or a worn tendon attachment — and each one needs a different approach.

Not One Diagnosis — Seven Different Problems

"Tennis elbow," "golf elbow," and "elbow arthritis" only tell you where the pain started or what position it shows up in — not what tissue is actually behind it. The same complaint, "elbow pain," can mean a knotted muscle, a compressed nerve, a stiff joint, poor local circulation, a worn-down tendon attachment, a strained ligament, or a problem that isn't really in the elbow at all.

At Greenleaf, we don't treat "elbow pain" as a single condition. We check which tissue is involved and how it's moving before deciding on an approach — because the right treatment for a tight forearm muscle is not the right treatment for a degenerated tendon attachment.

The 7 Elbow Pain Patterns

Recognizing which pattern fits your symptoms helps explain why treatment looks different from case to case.

01

Muscle Tightness & Overuse

Signs: Pressing on the forearm muscles (the ones that bend or extend the wrist) reproduces a dull ache that radiates toward the elbow; symptoms worsen after repetitive wrist movement — mouse use, housework, repeated gripping.

Approach: Acupuncture directly into the tight muscle to release it and restore local blood flow. We check the forearm muscles first, not just the elbow itself.

02

Nerve Irritation with Numbness

Signs: Long-standing tightness, or numbness and radiating pain into the fingers or hand; a tingling, electric sensation in certain positions.

Approach: Electroacupuncture to calm deep tension and reduce nerve sensitivity. If numbness persists, imaging may be needed to rule out nerve compression.

03

Restricted Range & Tissue Adhesion

Signs: The elbow won't fully straighten or bend; the forearm catches or hurts when rotating. Often follows trauma, surgery, or long-standing chronic pain.

Approach: Acupotomy to mechanically release adhered tissue and help restore range of motion.

04

Poor Local Circulation & Congestion

Signs: The elbow feels heavy, locally congested, or swollen — including swelling at the back of the elbow (the bursa); pain that's chronic and recurring.

Approach: Wet Cupping to draw out stagnant blood and support circulation and drainage of inflammatory byproducts. Temporary bruising is common afterward.

05

Tendon Attachment Degeneration (Chronic Tennis / Golf Elbow)

Signs: Tenderness directly over the bony bump on the outside (tennis elbow) or inside (golf elbow) of the elbow; pain when gripping or lifting; weakened grip strength.

Approach: Tendon heals slowly because it has less blood supply than muscle. Once it's chronic, simply relaxing the muscle isn't enough — we combine Acupotomy and Wet Cupping with regular and electroacupuncture to work directly on the degenerated tissue.

06

Joint Surface & Ligament Injury

Signs: Pain from within the joint itself when moving the arm in a specific direction; tenderness directly over a ligament.

Approach: Acupotomy to reduce pressure in a narrowed joint space and release adhered tissue around the joint capsule and ligaments.

07

Referred Pain from Wrist, Shoulder, or Neck

Signs: Recurring elbow pain despite a normal elbow exam; pins-and-needles toward the elbow when moving the neck or shoulder.

Approach: We check the wrist, shoulder, and neck alongside the elbow — more on this below.

Why Tennis Elbow & Golf Elbow Take So Long to Heal

Tennis elbow and golf elbow aren't simple muscle soreness — the core issue is degenerative change at the point where a tendon attaches to bone. Tendon has a much smaller blood supply than muscle, so it heals slowly. Early on, it's mostly inflammation; but repeated strain over time causes the tissue itself to weaken structurally. At that stage, structural recovery of the attachment point matters more than chasing inflammation, which is why pain that eases briefly tends to come back without it — treatment usually needs several weeks to several months of consistency.

Tennis Elbow

Outside of the elbow

The tendon of the wrist-extending muscles is damaged by repeated strain. Pain appears when lifting objects, extending the wrist upward, or shaking hands.

Golf Elbow

Inside of the elbow

The tendon of the wrist-flexing muscles is damaged by repeated strain. Pain appears when gripping objects or rotating the arm inward, along with weakened grip.

Why We Don't Just Look at the Elbow

The elbow sits in the middle of a movement chain that runs from the wrist and hand through the shoulder blade, shoulder, and neck. To find out why the elbow keeps getting overloaded, we check the joints above and below it too.

1

Wrist & Hand

A stiff wrist or weak grip forces the elbow muscles to compensate and work harder than they should.

2

Shoulder & Shoulder Blade

An unstable shoulder blade or tight shoulder muscles make the elbow overcompensate when reaching or lifting.

3

Neck & Upper Body

A compressed neck nerve can refer pain or numbness down into the elbow and forearm. Forward-head posture and rounded shoulders throw off alignment through the whole arm and load the elbow unevenly.

In practice, we check for tenderness and restricted movement in this order: wrist/finger muscles → elbow → shoulder → shoulder blade → neck — and when needed, we treat the shoulder and neck alongside the elbow to release tension through the whole chain.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many acupuncture sessions does elbow pain usually need?

Simple muscle tightness or a mild strain often improves within 1–3 sessions. Tendon attachment problems like tennis or golf elbow usually need 1–2 sessions a week for 8–12 weeks or more, and chronic cases with structural tissue change can take several months.

Is Acupotomy necessary for tennis elbow?

In the early, acute stage, regular acupuncture, electroacupuncture, and rest alone can be enough. But once it's become chronic and fibrosis or adhesion has set in, working directly on the affected tissue tends to be more effective. Releasing the muscle alone at that stage often brings only temporary relief before symptoms return.

Is Acupotomy safe?

Acupotomy is a form of acupuncture treatment, not surgery. A fine needle with a small blade edge releases adhered tissue without requiring a skin incision, stitches, or local anesthesia. Some soreness or bruising can follow and is usually temporary. Because the elbow area has many nerves and blood vessels nearby, it's important to have this done by a practitioner with solid anatomical training.

How long does bruising from Wet Cupping last?

Bruising typically fades within 3–10 days, though this varies by person. Heavier bruising in a specific spot often reflects poorer local circulation there. We recommend avoiding heavy alcohol, saunas, or hot baths for about 24 hours after treatment.

Can I keep exercising with elbow pain?

During an acute flare, avoid strenuous activity and stick to gentle stretching within a pain-free range. Once pain has settled, gradual forearm stretching and strengthening can help — the right timing and method are best worked out with your practitioner.

Do I need to treat my neck and shoulder too if it's just my elbow that hurts?

A large share of elbow pain is connected to the neck and shoulder. A compressed neck nerve, limited shoulder mobility, or an unstable shoulder blade can all place abnormal load on the elbow. Treating the elbow alone often brings only temporary relief, which is why checking the whole chain matters.

What should I do if numbness comes with my elbow pain?

Numbness or tingling into the fingers or hand can point to nerve compression, possibly originating in the neck. This calls for a different approach than simple muscle pain, and if it persists, imaging is recommended to properly evaluate it.

Can I get steroid injections alongside acupuncture treatment?

Steroid injections reduce inflammation strongly, but repeated use can weaken the tendon over time. Acupuncture supports blood flow and tissue release without weakening tissue, and can be combined with injections. Where possible, we recommend working toward genuine tissue recovery rather than relying on repeated injections.
