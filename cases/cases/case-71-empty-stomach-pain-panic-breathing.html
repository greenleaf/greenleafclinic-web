Stomach Pain on an Empty Stomach — and Then, One Day, Breathing Became Hard

Improvement over 4 weeks Mental Health Digestive Issues

On the morning commute, before she has eaten anything, her stomach begins to burn and ache. It has been going on for more than half a year, so at first she assumed it was gastritis. She took the stomach medication from her doctor faithfully, but the pain stayed the same. Then, two weeks ago, as work piled up all at once, her abdomen began to fill with gas until her waistband felt tight, and day after day she left the bathroom feeling she hadn't fully emptied.

What unsettled her more was her chest. Even sitting still, she could feel her heart pounding, and when she tried to take a deep breath, it seemed to catch somewhere high in her chest. At night her body was exhausted, but her mind stayed wide awake and sleep wouldn't come. She needed to urinate more often, and heat would suddenly rush up into her face and upper body for no clear reason.

This patient, a woman in her 30s, came to the clinic complaining of a stomach problem. Taken at face value, that would have called for treating the stomach. But as we went through her symptoms one by one, a different picture emerged.

Why the Stomach Medication Didn't Work: The Problem Started in the Autonomic Nervous System, Not the Stomach

Stomach pain, heartburn, bloating, constipation, palpitations, shortness of breath, trouble falling asleep, frequent urination, hot flashes. Viewed separately, they look like problems in different organs, but they share one common pathway: the autonomic nervous system that responds to stress.

When stress goes on for a long time, the body cannot switch off its sympathetic "alert mode." In this state the heart rate rises, breathing becomes shallow and fast, urination becomes more frequent, and temperature regulation wavers, producing sudden waves of heat. Meanwhile, activity of the parasympathetic nervous system (the vagus nerve), which governs digestion, drops. Gastric movement becomes irregular, blood flow that protects the stomach lining decreases, and the bowel slows down, causing gas and constipation. Pain on an empty stomach often reflects a stomach lining that, in this environment, can no longer tolerate the irritation of stomach acid.

The brain and gut are in constant communication through the vagus nerve. That is why a panic response often begins not in the chest but in the gut. Stomach medication can reduce acid, but it cannot release the nervous-system tension that put the stomach in that state. That is why her medication had no effect.

What the "Can't Get a Breath" Feeling Really Was: Hypertonic Muscles in the Neck and Upper Chest

When I pressed along her neck and upper chest during the exam, the pectoralis minor (a deep muscle in the upper chest just below the collarbone), the sternocleidomastoid (the neck muscle running diagonally from behind the ear down to the collarbone), and the scalenes (deep muscles on the side of the neck) were all hard and knotted. These muscles do very little in normal breathing. They are "accessory respiratory muscles" that lift the ribs to help when we are short of breath.

When tension persists, the body stops breathing deeply with the diaphragm and instead takes quick, shallow breaths using these accessory muscles. Overworked, they stiffen, and stiff muscles create a sensation of the chest being squeezed. That makes it feel like there isn't enough air, which leads to even shallower, faster breathing — and this in turn amplifies palpitations and anxiety in a vicious cycle. The feeling of her breath "catching" came largely not from her lungs or heart themselves, but from the tension in these muscles.

Treatment Approach: Releasing Tight Breathing Muscles and Calming an Overheated Stress Response

Treatment followed two tracks.

Acupuncture, twice a week, targeted the knotted accessory breathing muscles directly. When a needle reaches a hard spot in the pectoralis minor below the collarbone, or in the sternocleidomastoid and scalenes at the side of the neck, the muscle gives a brief twitch and then gradually softens under the fingertips. As the tightly bound upper chest loosens, the breath slowly begins to drop lower. When breathing returns to the diaphragm, the vagus nerve becomes active again, and heart rate and gut movement begin to settle along with it.

The herbal formula focused on calming the stress-response axis. It was chosen to settle excessive sympathetic arousal, reduce palpitations and anxiety, and address both the difficulty falling asleep and the sluggish gut. The key was not to treat the stomach in one place and the heart in another, but to address the root of all these symptoms — an overactive nervous system — at once.

After 4 Weeks: Digestive and Panic Symptoms Resolved Together

After four weeks of twice-weekly acupuncture and herbal medicine, the empty-stomach pain and heartburn, the bloating and constipation, the palpitations and shortness of breath, and the trouble falling asleep had all resolved. The heart of this case is that the digestive symptoms and panic symptoms did not resolve separately — they resolved together.

At the end of treatment, the patient said she had never imagined her symptoms might be related to panic. Panic disorder is widely known only as "sudden attacks where you can't breathe and feel like you're dying," but it is not uncommon for it to show up first, as it did here, through chronic stomach pain, indigestion, gas, and constipation. If digestive symptoms don't improve with stomach medication and are accompanied by palpitations, shortness of breath, and sleep problems, it may be worth looking at the autonomic nervous system before the stomach.
